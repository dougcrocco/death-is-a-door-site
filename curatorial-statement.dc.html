Curatorial Statement — Death Is a Door · CC Jasper
Curatorial statement

Death Is a Door

The room between two worlds
Curated by Doug Crocco

A door is not an ending. It is the last architecture of the living — a frame we build around the one passage we cannot see through. Every culture builds one for its dead: a ferry, a gate, a veil, a flight of stairs into water. The passage is never the destination. It is the interval, the held breath between what was and what comes after. Death Is a Door gathers painting, sculpture, photography and moving image made inside that interval — work that lingers in the room between two worlds rather than choosing a side.

The closed door

The exhibition begins with a door that cannot be opened and cannot be ignored. Doug Crocco’s Death Is a Door shows crosses painted over corrosion and the chain that keeps the living out and the dead in — the show’s founding image and its plainest question: what waits on the other side? Meredith Sellers answers by refusing to show us. In Empty (After Holbein), Holbein’s entombed Christ is cropped down to the feet and everything else is sealed under flat rose paint; the aperture holds only what the eye would reach last, a door left ajar exactly wide enough to see that no one is behind it.

The crossing

Every mythology keeps a boatman. In unitedfutures’ The Isle of the Dead 2026, after Arnold Böcklin, a shrouded passenger is rowed toward a shore of cypress and stone, and the crossing is unhurried, almost tender — transition as passage, not punishment. Pandele Radu’s Down the River removes even the boatman. A coffin rides a red current past the mountains toward a pale, open light, with no oar and no mourners; the river does the work, and the river does not come back.

Those left at the threshold

Some works stay with the living. Ellen R Hanson’s Funeral Flowers paints white flowers across a coffin from a photograph and blurs them until the moment slips its grip — grief and memory work the same way, softening the harder we look. Benjamin Cowan’s Pigeon Feather, Stone, Giotto Crying Angels brings back Giotto’s mourning angels, the first figures in Western painting to grieve like people, on a leaning wedge propped on a feather and a stone: grief balanced on the lightest possible thing. Henry Swanson’s Mask from Dallas wears a skull as a face, tied on with string beneath a broad red hat, the living body still visible at its edges. Like the Day of the Dead pilgrimages to the graves of those we have lost, it carries family bonds across the threshold rather than ending them there.

Ripeness and time

Beauty and decay share the same frame, because at the door they are the same event. Aramis Gutierrez’s Bletting Bananas and Mangos nests blackened fruit in flowers at full bloom — the oldest painterly warning, vanitas, rendered without judgement. Ripeness and rot are not opposites; they are the same time on a clock. Charlie Goering’s Three-Tiered Camera Obscura, the smallest work in the show, reduces that clock to a diagram of belief: a devil balancing on a shaft of light thrown from a clock tower, three crosses and a half-read clock face below.

The struggle at the door

Not every crossing is calm. Tom DeLaney’s Rage and Eternal shows a body twisting through fire, wings of shadow closing behind it, the flesh lit like hot metal — rage against the passage, and the eternity waiting beyond it. Sellers returns with Desire (After Dieric Bouts): two windows cut into one teal field, the crucified hand at one end and the serpent at the other, coiled and intact on its stone. The victory and the thing it was won against are held in tension across a surface that will not let them meet.

Vessels, stairs and shrines

Other works build the passage themselves. L’s Spell for blue light = 222D alignment suspends blue bulbs and blue lighters in oil — light above, flame below. Across traditions blue marks the threshold of the spirit; sealed in glass, it becomes a vessel that holds the light until the soul is ready to pass through. Kenny Curwood’s brass The World of Symbol Vanishes in the World as God climbs a staircase out of a scalloped cloud toward an eye whose pupil is an arched doorway. Held against the sky the sculpture is mostly air; the ascent is something you look through, and the door looks back. Megan Lindeman’s New eNightened Era, Coming Soon sets a promise in an arched niche — part doorway, part headstone, part empty shrine — written in the grammar of a shopfront between tenants. Faith and marketing speak the same tense: coming soon.

The dark night

The exhibition closes at its largest scale. In Charlie Goering’s Metamorphosis, or The Dark Night of the Soul, a skeleton sleeps curled against the rim of a star-scored globe, arms folded under its skull like any tired body. The night is not above it; it is the thing it lies on. At seventy-two inches square, the door here is the size of a room.

The subjects of these works are not death itself but its thresholds — the time of the minutes, days and weeks after; the feeling one holds for what was; the echo of stillness that occupies the void; the darkness that leads to the next reality. To stand before them is to stand at the threshold, and to be asked, gently, to look through.
